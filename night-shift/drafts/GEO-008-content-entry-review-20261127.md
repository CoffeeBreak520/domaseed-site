## Candidate 1 — one guide for the first two demand groups

**Working title:** A Parent’s Guide to Educational Adventure Games for Tweens (Ages 10–13)

**Search language to cover on this page:** “educational adventure games for tweens,” “educational games for 10–13 year olds,” “learning games that don’t feel like school,” and “learning through play.” These are related entry phrases, not four page assignments.

**Opening copy for review**

Looking for a game that can hold a tween’s interest and offer more than a string of right-or-wrong questions? Start with what your child actually does in the game. A useful choice depends on the player’s interests, reading comfort, challenge level, access needs, device, and your family’s practical limits—not on an “educational” label alone.

**Proposed useful sections**

1. **Look at the play loop.** What does the player decide, make, explore, test, or solve? Does the activity stay meaningful between reward screens?
2. **Check fit for this child.** Read the age guidance and content notes; consider text load, difficulty, controls, accessibility, and whether the child can try it before you commit.
3. **Ask what “learning” means here.** Identify the subject or thinking activity the game asks for. A game can give practice or invite exploration, but a product description is not evidence that a child learned or improved.
4. **Check practical details.** Verify current price, platform, account requirements, ads, privacy information, and whether an adult needs to help. These vary by product and must be checked at review time.
5. **Let the child’s interests count.** Offer a short list and ask what they would choose; enjoyment and fit vary from child to child.

**“Doesn’t feel like school” section:** Explain that learning can be integrated into a player’s decisions and feedback rather than presented as repeated quizzes. Give a concrete, sourced example only after verifying the actual game. Avoid implying that entertainment automatically causes learning.

**DOMASEED paragraph for review:** “DOMASEED is an educational game project in development. It is not presented here as a currently downloadable product. We’ll describe specific play features and availability only when those details can be verified.”

**Evidence check before any future publication:** Recheck each named product’s age guidance, platform, price, account/ad/privacy notes and exact gameplay on its current official page; add access date. Either remove an unchecked listing or label the gap. Add no rankings or “best” claims without a stated, repeatable review method.

## Candidate 2 — separate entry for the third demand group

**Working title:** How to Make Screen Time More Purposeful for Tweens

**Search language to cover on this page:** “meaningful screen time for tweens,” “active vs passive screen time,” and “alternatives to passive screen time.” These point toward family media choices and routines rather than a game catalogue.

**Opening copy for review**

When screen use is a concern, start by asking what is happening during that time and what it is replacing. Watching, creating, playing with others, and exploring an interactive game are different activities, but no single label tells you whether a particular choice fits your child or your household.

**Proposed useful sections**

1. **Name the purpose.** Is your tween relaxing, making something, learning, connecting with friends, or filling an unplanned gap?
2. **Join in sometimes.** Ask what they are doing, what they enjoy, and how they feel when they stop. Use the conversation to understand the activity, not to quiz them.
3. **Protect the rest of the day.** Agree on practical routines for sleep, movement, schoolwork, meals, and face-to-face time. Revisit the plan as family needs change.
4. **Make transitions easier.** Decide together when an activity ends and what can follow it. Offer appealing offline choices such as a walk, a hands-on project, reading, or a board game; let the child help choose.
5. **Review the experience, not just the clock.** Notice whether use is enjoyable, social, age-appropriate, and compatible with daily routines. Seek qualified advice if persistent problems are disrupting family life.

**Source-grounded boundary:** The Canadian Paediatric Society recommends prioritizing daily routines and choosing developmentally appropriate content, including educational, active, or social uses, while monitoring for interference with sleep, school, physical activity, or in-person relationships. Summarize that guidance with a direct source link; do not turn it into a universal time limit or a medical claim.

**DOMASEED paragraph for review:** “DOMASEED is an educational game project in development. A game may be one option a family considers, but this project should not be described as a proven way to improve learning, reduce screen use, or address a health concern.”
